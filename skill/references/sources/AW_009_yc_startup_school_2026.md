## Statement-type separation

### Direct statements by Wang

Wang describes YC as supportive while also bluntly telling founders when their thinking is poor. He says that after one or two months working on an AI medical-care agent, YC partner Jared Friedman told the founders he did not think the idea would go anywhere. Wang says this was exactly what they needed to hear. They returned to the drawing board, reconsidered the opportunity, and developed Scale's data-focused idea.

Wang explains the data bottleneck from his own model-training experience: compute and training code were readily accessible, but acquiring training data was not. He says founders need conviction in beliefs that are not yet consensus, must avoid allowing the surrounding herd to determine business decisions, and should develop an internal compass about the future.

He says nobody begins already skilled at starting a company and frames the task as continuously improving and learning quickly. He recommends identifying an exponential trend with both a steep curve and a long duration, even when its starting point looks unimportant.

On agents, Wang describes companies as feedback loops and says effective agentic systems require the right loop, evaluation, and optimization metric. He treats the metric as a mechanical core rather than magic.

### Interviewer premises

Garry Tan explicitly calls the Scale pivot a case study in first-principles thinking. That label originates with the interviewer. Wang agrees and then supplies his own explanation about uncommon convictions and an internal compass.

Tan also frames several questions about changes in computer science, systems thinking, and agent orchestration. Preserve those prompts as interviewer framing rather than Wang's independent agenda.

### Derived interpretations — not direct claims

- The episode supports willingness to accept blunt criticism when it exposes a weak idea, followed by reconsideration rather than defensiveness.
- The medical-agent episode is a failed or mistimed direction, not evidence that Wang treats all negative feedback as correct.
- The internal-compass principle coexists with accepting feedback: outside input can invalidate an implementation while first-principles experience supports the revised direction.
